# PART XV — THE ENTERTAINMENT SHORT FILM

> **This module overrides the commercial instinct in every other part of the manual.**
> Parts I–XIV were written for films that sell something. They still teach the craft — the moments-not-mood doctrine, the character locks, the environment engine, the sound policy — and every word of that still binds. What does NOT bind here is the selling. There is no brand, no product, no offering, no pitch, no tagline, no call to action, no logo and no end card in this film. If a rule elsewhere in the manual says "the product is the hero", it does not apply. **The STORY is the hero.**

## 15.1 WHAT THIS IS, AND WHAT IT IS COMPETING WITH

Someone paid real money for sixty seconds. They did not buy sixty seconds of atmosphere. They bought a **story they would tell someone else about.**

The film is going to be watched on a phone, in a feed, next to everything else on the internet. It has no brand to justify it and no client to satisfy — the ONLY thing that keeps a viewer watching is that they want to know what happens next. That is the entire job. A short film that is beautiful and about nothing is a failure of a kind an ad can survive and this cannot.

The bar: **would a stranger send this to a friend?** If the honest answer is "it's nice", the film is dead. Nice is not a reason to share something.

## 15.2 A COMPLETE STORY, INSIDE THE RUN-TIME

This is the law that separates a short film from a clip.

> **A story with no ending is not a short story. It is a fragment.**

Sixty seconds is not "a scene from a film". It is a whole film. It has a beginning, a middle and an end, and the end must actually happen ON SCREEN, inside the run-time, in the final scene. Not implied. Not left open. Not resolved by a caption. **Something is different at the end than it was at the start, and we watch it become different.**

The five obligations, in order. Every film owes all five:

| Obligation | What it means | Where it lives |
|---|---|---|
| **1. THE HOOK** | Something is already wrong / strange / funny / urgent in the first frame. | Scene 1, first 3 seconds |
| **2. THE WANT** | We learn who this is and what they are trying to get, without anybody explaining it. | Scene 1–2 |
| **3. THE TURN** | The thing that makes it impossible to go back. The story stops being what we thought it was. | The middle |
| **4. THE CLIMAX** | The single biggest moment. The confrontation, the decision, the reveal, the collision. | The last third |
| **5. THE LANDING** | The outcome. What that cost, what it earned, who they are now. Visible, physical, on screen. | The final scene |

**A 60-second film has SIX scenes for all five.** That is not tight, it is normal — the mistake writers make is thinking they need more time. They do not. They need to start later and cut harder. Open after the setup has already happened. A film that spends its first scene introducing someone has thrown away a sixth of itself.

## 15.3 THE HOOK IS THE WHOLE FILM'S RENT

The first three seconds decide whether the other fifty-seven exist. The thumb is already moving.

**Banned openings** — each one of these is a lost viewer:
- An establishing shot of a place.
- Someone walking into frame to begin.
- A landscape, a sunrise, a skyline, a drone move.
- A person waking up.
- Someone sitting down to think about something.
- A title card.

**What an opening frame does instead:** it starts on an event already in progress, and it raises a question the viewer needs answered.

| ✗ Dead open | ✓ Alive open |
|---|---|
| A fisherman walks down to his boat at dawn. | A fisherman is already waist-deep in water, dragging a net that is far too heavy, and something in it is moving. |
| A woman sits at her kitchen table looking worried. | A woman is stuffing banknotes into a rice tin, fast, and a key is turning in the door. |
| Two friends meet at a café. | One friend is already halfway through an apology the other is clearly not accepting. |

The question does not have to be a mystery. "Why is she hiding money?" is a question. "What is in the net?" is a question. "What did he do?" is a question. **No question, no film.**

## 15.4 STAKES: WHY WE CARE

Stakes are not scale. A stolen fishing boat is not automatically more interesting than a borrowed dress. Stakes are **what this person loses if it goes wrong, in their own life, on their own terms.**

Three things make a stake work:
1. **It is specific.** Not "her future" — the school fees, this Friday.
2. **It belongs to a person we can see wanting it.** Wanting is physical: reaching, checking, hiding, counting, going back for it.
3. **It can actually be lost.** A story where nothing could have gone wrong has no stakes, it has a schedule.

**The domestic scale is the strongest scale.** A grandmother's approval, a debt to a neighbour, a promise to a child, a lie that is about to be found out, a thing borrowed without asking. These render beautifully, cost nothing, and every human on earth has felt them. Reach for a disaster only when the story genuinely is one.

## 15.5 THE TURN

The middle of a short film is where most of them die. The writer has a good opening and a good ending and fills the gap with more of the opening.

The turn is the fix. It is the moment the story **stops being the story you thought it was.** Not a new event — a new SITUATION.

Turns that work in ten seconds:
- **The thing that was hidden is seen.** Somebody walks in.
- **The plan works, and that is the problem.**
- **The person we were rooting against turns out to be right.**
- **The helper becomes the obstacle.**
- **What they wanted arrives, and it is not what they wanted.**
- **The cost is revealed.** It was always going to cost this. Now we know what.
- **Somebody else wants it too**, and they are better at getting it.

After the turn, **the film must be running faster than before it**, not slower. If the back half of your film is calmer than the front half, you have written a fizzle.

## 15.6 THE ENDING — LAND IT, DO NOT DRIFT OUT OF IT

The single most common failure in AI short film: the last scene is a mood. Somebody smiles. The camera pulls back. Nothing is resolved and the film simply stops.

**The final scene is an EVENT, exactly like every other scene.** It carries the same beat count as the rest of the film. It shows the outcome physically:

| ✗ Drift | ✓ Landing |
|---|---|
| She smiles, the camera pulls back, we fade out. | She puts the empty tin back on the shelf, turns it label-in so the dent doesn't show, and her daughter — who has been watching from the doorway the whole time — hands her the last note. |
| He looks out at the sea, at peace with it. | He drops the net over the side, empty, and starts the engine anyway. The boat pulls away leaving the buyer standing on the jetty with his money still in his hand. |
| They hug and it's heartwarming. | She takes the dress back off the line, folds it, and puts it through the neighbour's window without knocking. |

Legitimate endings: the win, the loss, the price, the reversal, the return-changed, the deliberate cruelty of getting exactly what you asked for. **Not** legitimate: a summary, a smile, a look, a fade, "and life goes on".

**The last beat is the one they remember.** Spend it.

## 15.7 DIALOGUE IN A SHORT FILM

People speak on camera in this film. That is a tool, and it is the fastest one available — but it is also the fastest way to kill a short film, because a character who explains the story is a character nobody believes.

- **Nobody says the theme.** Not once. If a line could be printed on a poster, cut it.
- **People talk past each other.** Real dialogue is interruption, deflection, changing the subject, answering a different question.
- **Under ten words a line**, almost always. Ten seconds of screen time holds far less speech than you think, and the picture is doing most of the work.
- **The best line in a scene is often the one nobody says.** Somebody starts to answer and doesn't.
- **No exposition.** "Ever since Father died and left us the shop…" is the sound of a film dying. We work it out from what we see.
- **Silence is allowed and is often stronger.** A scene with no dialogue at all is a legitimate, frequently superior choice.

## 15.8 THE CAST OF A STORY

Everything in Part III and Part XII binds unchanged: locked character blocks, the complexion/hair/build spread, no two people alike, background crowds varied. The one difference:

**A short film has characters, not demographics.** An ad casts "a young mother", a story casts *this* woman, who does this specific thing with her hands when she is lying. Give every recurring character ONE behavioural tell — a physical habit that repeats and that the audience learns to read. It costs nothing in the word budget and it is the difference between a person and a model.

Two to four people is the right size for a sixty-second film. One is possible if the story is genuinely internal-made-external. Five is a crowd and nobody registers.

## 15.9 WHAT DOES NOT BELONG IN THIS FILM

Delete on sight, at every stage:

- Any product being sold, held up, hero-shot, or turned label-out to camera.
- Any logo, wordmark, brand card, end card, title card or closing plate.
- Any tagline, slogan, strapline or call to action.
- A narrator explaining the film's meaning.
- A voiceover of any kind (this film's words are spoken by its characters, on camera).
- The words "customer", "client", "offering", "solution", "brand".
- Any scene whose PURPOSE is "proof", "benefit", "demonstration" or "the brand lands".
- A closing shot that exists to summarise rather than to resolve.

A prop the story genuinely needs — a tin, a phone, a dress, a boat, a bag of rice — is not a product. It is an object in a story, described exactly once in the registry as an ELEMENT, kept consistent, and never presented. The test: **is the camera showing it to the characters, or showing it to the audience?** If it is being shown to the audience, it is an ad shot. Cut it.

## 15.10 THE DENSITY LAW STILL BINDS — AND HARDER

Everything in the density law applies to a short film exactly as it applies to an ad: at least three distinct beats in every ten seconds, five where you can, each one a change of state with its own physical verb.

The reason it matters MORE here: an ad can survive a slow scene because the viewer already knows why they are watching. A story cannot. A dead ten seconds in a short film is ten seconds in which the viewer decides they do not need to know how this ends.

A longer run-time is not permission to slow down. A 180-second film is eighteen scenes that all have to earn their place — it means a bigger story, more turns and more people, **never** the same story told more slowly.

## 15.11 THE "WOULD ANYONE CARE?" TEST

Run it on the storyline before a single scene is built:

1. **Say the film in one sentence, out loud.** If the sentence is a situation ("a family runs a shop") rather than a story ("a boy sells his father's shop radio to pay a debt he did not make"), it is not a film yet.
2. **What does the lead WANT?** If you cannot say it in four words, the audience cannot either.
3. **What goes wrong?** If nothing does, there is no film.
4. **What is different at the end?** If the answer is "they feel better", there is no film.
5. **What is the last image?** If it is a smile or a landscape, you have drifted out instead of landing.
6. **Who would repeat this to a friend, and what would they say?** Write that sentence. If you cannot, start again.
