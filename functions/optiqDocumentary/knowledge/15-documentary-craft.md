# OPTIQ KNOWLEDGE — PART XV: DOCUMENTARY CRAFT (the argument, the evidence, the complication, the close)
(Internal operating doctrine for the Optiq Documentary agent swarm. Loaded ONLY for the DOCUMENTARY film kind.)

# PART XV — THE DOCUMENTARY

> **This module overrides both the commercial instinct and the dramatic instinct in every other part of the manual.**
> Parts I–XIII were written for films that sell something, whose characters speak on camera. They still teach the craft — the moments-not-mood doctrine, the environment engine, the density law, the sound policy — and every word of that still binds. What does NOT bind here is the selling, and what does not bind here is the speech. There is no brand, no product, no offering, no pitch, no tagline, no logo and no end card in this film. There is also no dialogue, no interview and no talking head. If a rule elsewhere says "the product is the hero", it does not apply. If a rule elsewhere tells you how to write a line of dialogue, it does not apply. **The SUBJECT is the hero, and the film's only words are narration laid over it afterwards.**

## 15.1 WHAT THIS IS, AND WHAT IT IS COMPETING WITH

Someone paid real money for sixty seconds. They did not buy sixty seconds of beautiful footage of somewhere nice. They bought **something they did not know, shown to them.**

This film is going to be watched on a phone, in a feed, next to everything else on the internet. It has no brand to justify it and no plot to pull anyone through it. The only thing keeping a viewer watching is that it keeps showing them something they had not seen, and keeps implying there is more.

The bar: **would a stranger say "I didn't know that" out loud?** If the honest answer is "it looked nice", the film is dead. Nice is not information.

## 15.2 A DOCUMENTARY IS AN ARGUMENT, NOT A SUBJECT

This is the law that separates a documentary from a showreel.

> **A film that is ABOUT something instead of SAYING something is a location with a camera in it.**

"A documentary about the fish market" is not a film. It is eight beautiful shots that could be played in any order, ending because the run-time ended. That interchangeability is the test — **shuffle your scenes; if it reads the same, you have a slideshow.**

What replaces the plot is a **thesis**: one sentence the film is going to land, which you could be wrong about.

| ✗ Topic | ✓ Thesis |
|---|---|
| How salt is made in The Gambia. | The hard part of salt is not making it. It is carrying it. |
| A day at the fish market. | The price of a fish is set before dawn, by three men, and nobody else in the market knows it. |
| Traditional weaving. | This loom outlasts the person using it, and everybody who touches it knows that. |
| The life of a boat mechanic. | Nothing here is replaced. Everything is repaired, and that is a skill nobody is learning. |

Write the thesis first. Every scene either builds toward it or is cut.

## 15.3 THE OBLIGATIONS

Every scene is tagged with the one it serves. All of these appear across the film:

| Act | What it means | Where it lives |
|---|---|---|
| **open** | An event already in progress in the first frame, raising a question. | Scene 1 only |
| **question** | The specific physical thing that makes the film's real question unavoidable. | Early |
| **context** | The one thing you must know to care. **Rationed.** | Early, at most a scene or two |
| **evidence** | Specific, particular, physical things that build the case. The body of the film. | The middle, most of it |
| **turn** | The complication. The cost, the disagreement, the thing being lost. | The back half |
| **close** | The thesis landed physically, on camera. | The final scene only |

**Context is where documentaries go to die.** The reflex is to explain before showing. Resist it: an audience will accept far less setup than you think, and every second of background is a second not spent on the thing itself. Show first. Explain in six words if you must.

## 15.4 THE OPEN IS THE WHOLE FILM'S RENT

The first three seconds decide whether the other fifty-seven exist. The thumb is already moving.

**Banned openings** — each one is a lost viewer:
- An establishing shot of a place.
- A drone rising over anything.
- A sunrise, a skyline, a landscape.
- Someone walking into frame to begin.
- A title card.

**What an opening frame does instead:** it starts on an event already in progress, and it raises a question.

| ✗ Dead open | ✓ Alive open |
|---|---|
| A wide shot of the salt flats at dawn. | Two hands break the crust on a salt pan and lift out a slab the size of a paving stone. |
| The market from above, bustling. | Ice going into a crate, shovel by shovel, faster than seems reasonable, at 4am. |
| A weaver sits down at his loom. | A thread snaps, and two hands are already splicing it before you see whose they are. |

The question does not need to be a mystery. "How heavy is that?" is a question. "Why is he doing it that way?" is a question. **No question, no film.**

## 15.5 EVIDENCE: THE PARTICULAR BEATS THE GENERAL, ALWAYS

The body of a documentary is evidence, and evidence is specific.

- Not "the workers carry heavy loads" — **this** woman, **this** basin, and the way she shifts her grip at the third step.
- Not "generations of craftsmanship" — **this** groove worn into **this** bench by **this** repeated motion.
- Not "the trade is under pressure" — the empty half of the shed, and the tools still hanging in it.

Two rules follow:
1. **No two evidence scenes may make the same point twice.** A second angle on the same idea is padding, and padding in a documentary reads as running out of material.
2. **Order is an argument.** Scene 4 is where it is because of what scene 3 showed. Build them so they depend on each other.

## 15.6 THE COMPLICATION

A documentary with no complication is a brochure. Somewhere in every real subject there is a cost, a disagreement, a thing being lost, or a part that does not fit — and finding it is the difference between a film and a tourism advert.

Complications that work in ten seconds:
- **The cost is visible.** Hands, backs, hours, damage.
- **Two people do the same job differently**, and one of them is right.
- **It is disappearing.** The empty space where the other five used to be.
- **The reward is smaller than the work.** Shown, not stated.
- **The easy way exists and is not being used**, for a reason.

After the complication, the film should feel like it is closing in, not widening out.

## 15.7 THE CLOSE — LAND IT, DO NOT FADE OUT OF IT

The single most common failure in AI documentary: the last scene is a wide shot at sunset and the film simply stops.

**The final scene is an EVENT, exactly like every other scene.** It carries the same beat count as the rest of the film and it lands the thesis physically:

| ✗ Drift | ✓ Close |
|---|---|
| A wide shot of the flats as the sun goes down. | The last slab goes onto the stack, she wipes her hands down her wrapper, and the stack is taller than she is. |
| The market empties, and life goes on. | The ice man tips the last of the crate into the drain, water and all, and pulls the shutter down. |
| The weaver smiles at his work. | He cuts the cloth off the loom, folds it once, and starts threading the next warp before the bench is cold. |

Legitimate closes: the thing finished, the thing leaving, the thing stopping, the thing starting again, the count completed, the light going off. **Not** legitimate: a summary, a smile, a fade, a drone pull-back, "and so life continues".

**The last beat is the one they remember.** Spend it.

## 15.8 THE PEOPLE IN A DOCUMENTARY

Everything in Part III and Part XII binds on *how* to describe a person: the keyword "Black", the complexion/hair/build spread, no two people alike, background crowds varied. What is different here is *who* gets described, and how often.

**A documentary has almost no recurring people.** It is about a place, a process, a system or a trade, and the people in each scene are whoever is doing that work in that ten seconds. They are written fresh inside their own scene, they carry no lock, and they never appear again. That is not a compromise — it is the format, and it is what makes a documentary feel found rather than staged. It is also why this sandbox renders no character reference sheets: there is nobody to keep consistent.

A film may follow ONE person all the way through, if the brief is genuinely about that person. Two is the ceiling. Past that it has quietly become a character drama with the dialogue removed, which is a different product on this platform.

Three rules that apply to everyone on camera:
1. **Nobody looks at the lens.** No posing, no smiling for the camera, no acknowledgement of being filmed.
2. **Nobody speaks.** See §15.9 and Part VII.
3. **Everyone is working.** Real workwear, marked by the job. Nobody in a documentary is dressed for the camera.

## 15.9 WHAT DOES NOT BELONG IN THIS FILM

Delete on sight, at every stage:

- Any product being sold, held up, hero-shot, or turned label-out to camera.
- Any logo, wordmark, brand card, end card, title card or closing plate.
- Any tagline, slogan, strapline or call to action.
- **Any dialogue.** Not a line, not a greeting, not a shout. Nobody speaks on camera.
- **Any interview or talking head.** The reflex the word "documentary" triggers, and unusable here: the footage carries no speech.
- **Any on-screen text.** No captions, lower thirds, names, dates, statistics, charts, labelled maps or subtitles. The video model renders text as garbled shapes.
- **Any invented fact.** No statistic, date, name, price or history that did not come from the director's brief or is not plainly visible. A documentary that makes things up is worse than one that says less.
- Archive footage, historical re-enactment, and anything that happened before the camera arrived. It cannot be shot. If it matters, the narration says it in one line.

An object the film looks at closely — a boat, a knife, a mould, a machine — is not a product. Looking at something honestly is the opposite of a hero shot. The test: **is the camera showing it as part of the world, or presenting it to the audience the way an advert does?** Presented, it is an ad shot. Cut it.

## 15.10 THE DENSITY LAW STILL BINDS — AND HARDER

Everything in the density law applies here exactly as it applies to an ad: at least three distinct beats in every ten seconds, five where you can, each a change of state with its own physical verb.

The reason it matters MORE here: a drama can survive a slow scene on a performance. **A silent illustrative clip has nothing to survive on but events.** Ten seconds of somebody standing thoughtfully in a doorway is ten seconds of nothing, whatever the narrator is saying over the top of it — and a narrator talking over nothing is the narrated slideshow this whole sandbox exists to prevent.

A longer run-time is not permission to slow down. A 180-second documentary is eighteen scenes that all have to earn their place: **a bigger argument, more evidence, a further complication — never the same argument told more slowly.**

## 15.11 THE SOUND-OFF TEST, AND THE OTHER ONE

Run both on the outline before a single scene is built.

**THE SOUND-OFF TEST.** Play the film silently in your head, with no narration at all. Does it still mean something? If it collapses without the voice, the pictures were never doing the work, and no amount of good narration will save it. Fix the pictures.

**THE "SO WHAT?" TEST.**
1. **Say the thesis out loud.** If it is a topic rather than a claim, it is not a film yet.
2. **What question does the film owe an answer to?**
3. **Name three specific, physical, particular things the film shows.** If they are all general impressions, there is no evidence.
4. **Where does it stop being simple?** If nowhere, it is a brochure.
5. **What physically happens in the last shot?** If the answer is a sunset or a smile, you have faded out instead of closing.
6. **What would a stranger repeat afterwards?** Write that sentence. If you cannot, start again.
