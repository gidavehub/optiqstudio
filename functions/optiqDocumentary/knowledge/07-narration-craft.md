# PART VII — NARRATION

> In the ad swarm and the story sandbox, Part VII teaches you how people talk on camera. **In a documentary nobody talks on camera at all.** Every word in the finished film is a narrator's voiceover, written here and recorded afterwards over the cut. This module replaces that one entirely.

## 7.1 HOW THE AUDIO ACTUALLY GETS MADE

Understanding the machine is most of the craft, because every rule below falls out of it.

1. **The clips are generated SILENT of speech.** The video model is told, three separate times per prompt, that nobody speaks and no lips move in speech. What comes back carries only diegetic sound and room tone.
2. **The clips are cut together.** Each one is nominally ten seconds and actually about eight; audio post measures every one of them rather than trusting the nominal length.
3. **Audio post WATCHES each clip** and reports, in that clip's own seconds, where a voice could sit — the stretches where the picture is settled and legible.
4. **The narration you wrote is fitted to those windows.** Each line gets a word budget computed from the real gap it has to live in. A line that is too long is rewritten shorter, automatically, and the machine picks which words to lose.
5. **A TTS narrator reads it**, and the takes are measured and re-fitted until they land inside their windows.
6. **Lyria 3 Pro composes the score** against the finished cut, and it is laid underneath.

Two consequences worth internalising:

- **A line over budget is not "a bit long". It is a line you no longer control.** Write short.
- **A shot where somebody's mouth is moving is unusable**, because the audience will hear a narrator and see somebody talking. This is why the no-speech rule is enforced as hard as the no-music rule.

## 7.2 THE WORD BUDGET

The narrator reads at roughly **2.5 words per second**, and a line sits inside a *gap* in the picture rather than running the whole ten seconds.

| | Words |
|---|---|
| Aim for, per scene | **~16** |
| Absolute ceiling, per scene | **24** |
| Legitimate, and often best | **0** |

Count the words. Then cut two.

## 7.3 THE ONE RULE THAT MATTERS MOST

> **Never describe what the viewer can already see.**

The picture is doing that. The narration exists to say what the picture *cannot*:

| The picture shows | ✗ Narration that wastes the slot | ✓ Narration that earns it |
|---|---|---|
| A woman lifting a heavy basin onto her head | "She lifts the heavy basin onto her head." | "Forty kilos. She'll do this eleven more times before noon." |
| Ice being shovelled into a crate | "Workers shovel ice into a crate." | "The ice costs more than the fish it's keeping." |
| A man splicing a snapped thread | "He repairs the broken thread." | "He learned this from his father. Nobody has asked to learn it from him." |

A narration line that describes the frame is a line the film would be better off without.

## 7.4 IT IS ONE PIECE, NOT CAPTIONS

Read the whole script back, start to finish, with no pictures. It has to sound like **one person thinking**, in order — not like a set of labels, one per shot.

- Line 4 continues line 3. If it could be moved to any other scene, it is a caption.
- Do not restate the thesis in every scene. State it once, late, and let the rest of the film point at it.
- Do not open by announcing the subject ("In The Gambia, salt has been made for centuries…"). The picture already told them where they are. Start in the middle of the thought.

## 7.5 SILENCE IS A LINE

**At least one or two scenes in every film carry no narration at all.**

A film talked over end to end is exhausting, and it tells the audience the pictures cannot carry themselves. The strongest moment in a documentary is usually the one the narrator shuts up for — and the close very often lands harder with no words on it, so the last thing the audience gets is the event itself.

Silence is also the only way the diegetic sound is ever heard. If a scene has a great sound in it — the crust cracking, the shutter coming down, the engine catching — take the words off it.

## 7.6 THE REGISTER

- **Plain spoken language.** Contractions. Say it the way you would say it to someone standing next to you.
- **Short sentences.** A sentence with two commas in it will not survive being read aloud into a four-second gap.
- **No advertising cliché.** No "unlock", "elevate", "journey", "game-changing", "in a world where", "little did they know".
- **No rhetorical questions.** "But what happens when the tide comes in?" is the sound of a film with nothing to say.
- **No exclamation marks.** The narrator is not excited; the subject is interesting.
- **Never read out text that is on screen** — and in this film there is none, so never write a line that only works as a caption.

## 7.7 FACTS

**Never invent one.** No statistic, date, name, price, quantity or history that did not come from the director's brief or is not plainly visible in the footage.

This is not pedantry. A documentary's entire value is that it is true, and a single fabricated number is the thing a viewer will notice, remember and repeat. If a figure would strengthen the film and you do not have it, either say the same thing without the number ("more than she can carry in one go") or leave the scene silent and tell the director what you would need.

## 7.8 CHOOSING THE VOICE

Audio post picks a narrator from the house voice catalogue and reads the film in that register. What the outline supplies is a short **narrator note** — under twenty words, describing how this specific film should be read: *"slow, plain, no warmth added — let the work speak"*, *"quick and curious, like someone who just found this out"*.

Match the voice to the subject, never to the medium. A film about hard physical work does not want a trailer voice.

## 7.9 THE CHECKLIST

Before an outline leaves your hands:

1. Is every line under 24 words? Count them.
2. Does any line describe what the picture already shows? Cut it or replace it.
3. Read the script end to end. Does it sound like one person, or like captions?
4. Are at least one or two scenes silent?
5. Is there a single fact in it you cannot point to a source for? Remove it.
6. Does the last line — or the deliberate absence of one — land the thesis?
